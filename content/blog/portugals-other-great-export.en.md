*Forget the souvenir shop. A more interesting Portugal is being made by artists who refuse to treat tradition as something that belongs behind glass.*

A porcelain factory, a wall stripped down to its hidden layers, a sculpture assembled from discarded objects. Three very different ideas of what Portuguese culture might look like now. None needs a lecture about how old the country is.

Portugal's artistic strength is not merely that it has traditions. It is that its best makers keep finding things to do with them.

## Joana Vasconcelos: a familiar object, seen differently

Joana Vasconcelos works at a scale that can turn something domestic into something impossible to ignore. Her practice draws on textiles, craft, ornament, popular culture and the objects of ordinary life, often in extravagant, humorous combinations. Her own account of her career ties that visual language to growing up in Portugal and to the country's traditions of tiles, ceramics, embroidery and jewelry.

It is tempting to describe the result as a bridge between high and low culture. But that makes it sound like a curator's exercise. The artworks are more alive than that. They ask why a familiar material should lose its capacity to surprise us simply because we have seen it in a house or a shop.

Her international exhibitions—from Versailles to Bilbao—are evidence that Portuguese visual culture can travel without disguising its origins.

## Vhils: the portrait inside the wall

Alexandre Farto, known as Vhils, often works by removing material. Where a conventional mural adds paint, his carved surfaces expose the accumulated skin of a building. Faces emerge from plaster, brick and the marks of earlier lives.

The method has a wonderful logic. A city is never a blank canvas, and neither is a person. His archive ranges across public walls, museums and unexpected materials, but the central thought remains legible: places store memories, including ones that redevelopment would prefer to cover.

For a country whose cities are being transformed by tourism, investment and migration, that is more than a pleasing aesthetic.

## Bordalo II: beautiful animals, uncomfortable materials

Artur Bordalo, known as Bordalo II, constructs giant animal forms from discarded plastics, metal and other refuse. You recognize the creature before you recognize what it is made from. Then the material becomes the argument.

The artist has explained the connection between consumption, waste and the environment. It is art with a visual punch and a deliberately awkward second thought: the materials that threaten animals are being used to give the animals a voice.

A child may love the sculpture immediately. An adult may begin to inspect their own habits. Good public art can reach both without asking either to read a wall label first.

## Beyond the art world

This sensibility reaches into Portugal's creative businesses, food culture, architecture and craftsmanship. The interesting question is not whether something is “traditional” or “modern.” It is whether the person making it understands the material, the place and the reason it should exist.

That is also a better definition of taste. Not a price tag. Not a fashionable address. Curiosity, discernment and enough confidence to recognize quality where you didn't expect to find it.

Start with one artist. Follow the materials. You may find that the most interesting thing Portugal exports is not an object at all, but an unusually productive conversation between old knowledge and new ideas.

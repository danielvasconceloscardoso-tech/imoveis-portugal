# The day Portugal changed its king

**Good morning, and welcome to the first Maré.**

Some national holidays are easy to explain: a declaration, a victory, a constitution. Portugal's December 1 is more cinematic. Picture a palace beside the Tagus, a political plot, and a hiding place that offered no protection whatsoever.

On **December 1, 1640**, a group of Portuguese nobles entered the Ribeira Palace in Lisbon to overthrow the representatives of the Spanish monarchy. Portugal had shared a crown with Spain for sixty years. The plotters went straight to where power lived.

**Margaret of Savoy, Duchess of Mantua**, the viceroy governing Portugal, was detained. Secretary of State **Miguel de Vasconcelos**, a deeply unpopular figure, tried to hide. The Portuguese Parliament recounts that the conspirators discovered him **inside a cupboard, behind a curtain**. He was killed during the takeover, and his body was thrown from a palace window to the crowd outside.

It's a startling episode, but the consequence matters more than the drama. The coup cleared the way for the Duke of Braganza to become **King João IV**, beginning a new chapter in Portugal's independence.

Now for the part most visitors miss. The Ribeira Palace is gone. The devastating earthquake of 1755 destroyed it; today's Praça do Comércio occupies the waterfront where the palace once stood. The enormous square feels open and orderly. Its earlier life was anything but.

A painter saw the dramatic potential. **Acácio Lino** portrayed the 1640 conspirators in a work associated with Portugal's Parliament, and the parliamentary museum preserves preparatory studies. The political event became a national memory. Then the memory became art.

Next time you cross Praça do Comércio, look toward the Tagus and imagine a palace where the square is now. Lisbon is especially good at hiding whole centuries in plain sight.
